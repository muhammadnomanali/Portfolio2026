Designing Smart Buying Starts With Structural Safety

This project details the design of a persuasive infographic, demonstrating how typography, color, and visual hierarchy function as rhetorical tools to guide consumer decision-making and convey complex data ethically.

Project Description

This project reflects on the creation of the infographic Smart Buying Starts With Structural Safety, designed for Dr. Stewart Whittemore’s ENGL 7080 course to persuade prospective car buyers to prioritize structural safety features. Rather than treating the infographic as a purely aesthetic artifact, I approached it as a complex rhetorical document where design functions as a primary carrier of meaning. The project demonstrates that documents convey meaning not only through text but through visual structure, typography, and layout. Ultimately, it highlights how careful document design shapes how audiences interpret information and act upon it in high-stakes consumer contexts.

Rhetorical and Technical Decisions

A primary technical challenge was structuring the infographic to allow buyers to quickly understand the relationship between demographic habits and safety. I made the rhetorical decision to organize the design into clearly segmented visual sections, using an age-based demographic dashboard as the structural backbone. Furthermore, I selected the Poppins typeface to establish a contemporary aesthetic and built a rigorous visual hierarchy—using 41.72 pt bold for primary headings down to 11 pt for body text—to guide the reader’s scanning pathways.

I also deployed a specific color palette featuring dark teal, forest green, golden yellow, and soft amber to create visual contrast that emphasizes critical data points without disrupting overall coherence. Finally, the infographic underwent iterative usability testing with a small group of viewers. This crucial technical step allowed me to evaluate clarity and revise confusing graphical elements based on direct audience feedback, ensuring the final document balanced visual appeal with informational clarity.

Theoretical Connections

To ground these design choices, I relied on Wyatt and DeVoss (2017), whose work on visual rhetoric establishes that design choices actively construct meaning rather than merely support it, with typography directly signaling importance and directing reading pathways. When segmenting the demographic data, I applied Cairo’s (2013) argument that information graphics must facilitate insight rather than simply display raw data, a principle that also justified my reliance on iterative, user-centered usability testing.

To ensure the demographic dashboard was efficient, I utilized Tufte’s (2001) emphasis on the clear presentation of quantitative information, noting how visual contrast critically influences interpretation in data-rich environments. While icons and charts successfully translated abstract safety considerations into concrete references, I remained attentive to the ethical concerns raised by Dragga and Voss (2001). Guided by their caution that technical illustrations can dehumanize or mislead when they obscure complexity, I consciously balanced informational clarity with ethical responsibility, ensuring the visual data remained accurate, proportional, and contextually grounded.
