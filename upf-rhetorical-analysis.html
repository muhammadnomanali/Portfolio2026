Rhetorical Analysis of Ultra-Processed Food Discourse

This project utilizes rhetorical analysis to examine how medical, corporate, advocacy, and regulatory stakeholders construct competing narratives and manage uncertainty around the health risks of ultra-processed foods.

Project Description

This research project, titled “Nutrient-Fortified or Metabolic Threat? A Rhetorical Analysis of Ultra-Processed Food Discourse,” investigates how various stakeholders frame the health risks of ultra-processed foods (UPFs) through strategic rhetoric rather than neutral scientific explanation. Developed for Dr. Susan Youngblood’s course on the rhetoric of risk and harm, the project explores the gap between public messaging about UPFs and their actual health implications. By analyzing the communicative practices of medical experts, corporate food actors, advocacy groups, and regulators, the paper demonstrates how these entities construct competing narratives. Ultimately, the project highlights how public perception, policy direction, and consumer behavior regarding food and health are actively shaped by these interacting, often conflicting, discourses.

Rhetorical and Technical Decisions

Methodologically, I made the rhetorical decision to employ a qualitative approach focused on discourse rather than attempting to determine the objective scientific safety of UPFs. To execute this, I selected a diverse range of artifacts across different discourse communities—including medical commentary, PepsiCo’s Nutrition Forward campaign, advocacy materials from the Center for Food Safety, and FDA guidance on “healthy” labeling. This selection allowed me to identify and contrast specific rhetorical strategies, such as the industry's use of strategic ambiguity and focus on affordability, versus advocacy groups' use of emotive framing and systemic urgency.

By analyzing these texts, I chose to highlight how uncertainty is managed rhetorically in public health debates. Furthermore, my technical decisions in structuring the analysis emphasized how food communication is deeply intercultural—shaped not just by data, but by issues of class, trust, and public values. Demonstrating that public understanding of UPFs emerges not from one single source of truth but from a struggle among competing rhetorical framings positions my work firmly within the scope of technical and professional communication (TPC).

Theoretical Connections

To ground this analysis, I relied on Gross’s (1990) foundational premise that public controversies are constituted through discourse rather than merely communicated by it. This principle guided my examination of how the UPF debate is actively constructed by its participants. When analyzing how stakeholders present, soften, or intensify the issue, I utilized Covello and Sandman's (2001) framework of risk communication, which clarifies that scientific uncertainty is frequently managed rhetorically rather than resolved through objective data.

As I synthesized the competing narratives of industry, advocacy, and regulation, Foucault’s (1972) theories on the productive nature of discourse helped explain how knowledge about UPFs is not neutral, but heavily shaped by underlying systems of power and institutional authority. To situate this research within my broader TPC portfolio, I applied Spinuzzi's (2008) concept of assemblage, framing the UPF debate as a meaning-making process produced through the complex interactions of various institutions, documents, technologies, and audiences. Finally, to address the intercultural aspects of food communication, I integrated Trauth’s (2015) research on community literacies and "natural" labels, which provided a theoretical basis for understanding how different communities unevenly interpret nutritional noise and health messaging.
