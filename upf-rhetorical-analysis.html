Nutrient-Fortified or Metabolic Threat?

A Rhetorical Analysis of Ultra-Processed Food Discourse

This project utilizes rhetorical analysis to examine how medical, corporate, advocacy, and regulatory stakeholders construct competing narratives and manage uncertainty around the health risks of ultra-processed foods.

  • Risk Communication

Introduction

In a 2021 systematic review published in Obesity Reviews, Lane et al. revealed that ultra-processed foods (UPFs) comprise 58% of calories consumed in the United States, coinciding with a 40% adult obesity rate. Yet food manufacturers insist these products are “nutrient-rich” and “affordable.” A significant gap persists between what is said about UPFs and what the epidemiological evidence reveals about their metabolic consequences. This discrepancy between concealed risk and constructed perception at the point of consumption is the central concern of this study. Consumers who encounter products marketed as “natural” or “healthy” tend to treat those items as benign, consuming them in greater quantities over extended periods—a pattern that contributes to weight gain and metabolic dysregulation precisely because the framing obscures the product’s actual nutritional profile.

This paper analyzes how stakeholders weaponize rhetoric to shape perceptions of UPF risks. Through a critical examination of marketing strategies, public health messaging, regulatory communications, and advocacy discourse, it uncovers the underlying narratives that contribute to the normalization of UPFs in daily diets. By deconstructing the language used in advertising, health guidelines, and activist campaigns, the analysis highlights the discrepancies that mislead consumers regarding their dietary choices and exposes the rhetorical mechanisms through which competing interests construct divergent accounts of the same products.

Ultimately, through appeals to scientific authority, strategic ambiguity, and emotive framing, medical experts, industry leaders, advocacy organizations, and regulatory bodies construct competing narratives about UPFs, revealing how risk communication influences policy and consumer behavior. This discourse not only shapes individual perceptions but also informs broader public health policies that may inadvertently support the continued prevalence of UPFs in the food supply. By employing a rhetorical framework to dissect these narratives, this study identifies contradictions within the discourse and proposes actionable recommendations for improving public health messaging.

Background and Exigency

Ultra-processed foods are defined within the NOVA classification system as food products that have undergone significant industrial processing, often containing additives such as preservatives, artificial colors, and flavors. As Temple (2024) explains, these foods are typically low in nutritional value, high in unhealthy ingredients, and contribute to various diet-related health issues. This classification raises important questions about the accuracy of health claims made by manufacturers, as many products marketed as “natural” or “healthy” contain misleading information. This complexity is compounded by marketing tactics that emphasize the convenience and affordability of UPFs, often overshadowing their potential health risks. Lockyer et al. (2023) emphasize that as public health guidelines increasingly grapple with the representation of UPFs, critically assessing the language used in these communications becomes essential for ensuring clarity and accuracy in consumer messaging.

This complexity is echoed by Ghosh-Jerath et al. (2024), who note that the lack of a standardized definition for UPFs in many regions further complicates efforts to create effective public health interventions and consumer education strategies. Furthermore, researchers such as Bonaccio et al. (2023) and Rico-Campà et al. (2019) argue that growing concerns about diet-related non-communicable diseases (NCDs) linked to UPF intake—including obesity, cardiovascular disease, and type 2 diabetes—make the rhetorical analysis of UPF discourse not merely an academic exercise but a matter of public health urgency.

The alarming rise in metabolic disorders contrasts sharply with the continued expansion of the ultra-processed food industry, which thrives on consumer demand for convenience and affordability. Hayashi and Takemi (2015) underscore that this juxtaposition creates an urgent need for effective public health interventions that can mitigate adverse health impacts while addressing the challenges posed by widespread availability and aggressive marketing. Consequently, Capewell and Lloyd-Williams (2022) contend that the increasing prevalence of these metabolic disorders calls for a critical reevaluation of food policies and marketing regulations that currently allow UPFs to dominate the market. This exigency demands a concerted effort from health authorities, policymakers, and the food industry to implement robust strategies aimed at reducing UPF consumption.

Literature Review

The consumption of ultra-processed foods poses significant health risks, including obesity, type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, and increased all-cause mortality. Research consistently demonstrates that UPFs, which are often high in added sugars, sodium, and unhealthy fats, contribute to metabolic dysregulation and weight gain. Validating these concerns, Lane et al. (2021) conducted a systematic review of 43 observational studies, finding that higher UPF consumption is associated with a 36% increased risk of obesity and a 28% higher risk of all-cause mortality. Similarly, Srour et al. (2020) demonstrated through a prospective cohort study in France that individuals with higher UPF intake face a 15% greater risk of developing type 2 diabetes. Silva et al. (2018) provide further support for these findings, linking UPFs to elevated body mass index and waist circumference independent of total calorie intake. Additionally, independent prospective cohort studies by Bonaccio et al. (2023) and Rico-Campà et al. (2019) firmly implicate UPFs in cardiovascular diseases, demonstrating a clear association between ultra-processed diets, metabolic syndrome, and increased mortality rates.

The mechanisms behind these risks include the promotion of overeating due to the hyper-palatability and convenience of UPFs, as well as their poor nutritional quality. Advocacy groups and medical experts emphasize the urgent need for stricter regulations, public health campaigns, and consumer education to mitigate these risks, while food manufacturers often downplay the dangers, focusing instead on affordability and convenience. This body of epidemiological evidence provides the foundation against which the rhetorical strategies of different stakeholders must be evaluated.

Methodology

To understand the discourse surrounding UPFs, this study employs a qualitative rhetorical analysis. The aim is not to settle the public health debate over whether UPFs are beneficial or harmful, but rather to assess the rhetorical strategies through which different stakeholders project or conceal particular aspects of UPF products. First, a content analysis of marketing materials and public health communications identifies recurring themes and rhetorical strategies. This is complemented by an examination of consumer-facing health blogs to ascertain common perceptions. By triangulating these data sources, the study develops a comprehensive understanding of the discourse surrounding UPFs and its implications for public health policy. The following artifacts represent four distinct stakeholder positions—medical, industry, advocacy, and regulatory—each analyzed for its rhetorical framing, target audience, evidentiary basis, and proposed solutions.

Findings: Competing Discourses

Medical Discourse

Perro and Adams (2017) present What’s Making Our Children Sick? as a paradigmatic instance of medical advocacy discourse in the UPF debate. The authors frame GMOs as inherently risky, using alarmist language (“hidden danger,” “toxic soup,” “nefarious”) and emphasizing health risks to children—autism, gut disruption, chronic inflammation—while omitting counterarguments about GMO safety or benefits such as drought-resistant crops. They prioritize anecdotal evidence from clinical practice alongside contested studies (e.g., Séralini’s rat study) but gloss over the methodological limitations of such research. The target audience is clearly parents and health-conscious consumers, leveraging emotional appeals over systemic policy solutions.

This discourse aligns with advocacy interests, portraying regulatory agencies as corrupt and dissenting scientists as silenced martyrs. The solutions proposed are individualistic—buy organic, detox homes—rather than regulatory, reflecting a distrust of institutional change. This tension between precautionary advocacy and evidentiary regulation recurs throughout the artifacts examined here, manifesting next in the radically different rhetorical register of corporate discourse.

Industry Discourse

PepsiCo’s 2023 Nutrition Forward campaign employs strategic rhetorical framing to present ultra-processed foods as nutritionally improved and socially responsible, while avoiding critical discourse on their health risks. The campaign uses promotional language and euphemisms like “convenient foods portfolio” to sidestep the term “ultra-processed,” focusing instead on reformulation efforts such as sodium reduction and nutrient fortification. Scholars such as Monteiro et al. (2019) describe this tactic as “nutri-washing,” where corporate messaging targets consumers, shareholders, and regulators by positioning the brand as a leader in health-conscious innovation.

By contrast, Pomeranz et al. (2023) illustrate how public health discourse highlights the metabolic risks of UPFs, specifically pointing to their hyper-palatability and displacement of whole foods. This divergence reveals a key tension: industry narratives prioritize incremental, market-friendly changes, while critics demand systemic shifts in food production and policy.

Advocacy Discourse

The Center for Food Safety's (2024) report frames UPFs as inherently harmful, using urgent, advocacy-driven language to highlight their risks. Terms like “toxic,” “industrial,” and “harmful” dominate the text, painting UPFs as products of corporate greed that threaten public health and the environment. As detailed by the Center for Food Safety (2024) in their anniversary report, this framing shifts the focus away from nuanced nutritional critiques and instead ties UPFs to broader systemic issues like factory farming and corporate control of food systems. The target audience is advocacy-minded: consumers, policymakers, and grassroots activists. The absence of the exact term “ultra-processed” in the explicit language is notable, suggesting that even critics often attack UPFs indirectly through adjacent issues like pesticides or GMOs.

Consumer Literacy Discourse

In the study Nutritional Noise: Community Literacies and the Movement Against Foods Labeled as “Natural”, Trauth (2015) critiques the unregulated use of “natural” labels on food products, indirectly addressing UPFs by highlighting misleading marketing claims. In her analysis, Trauth (2015) cites studies showing that “natural” labels create a “halo effect,” leading to overconsumption despite providing no actual health benefits. The paper exposes health risks by linking UPFs to obesity and metabolic disorders, citing peer-reviewed research and grassroots movements that challenge food industry practices. This artifact bridges the advocacy and regulatory domains, demonstrating how consumer literacy scholarship identifies the precise rhetorical gap that regulatory discourse must address.

Policy Advocacy Discourse

Roache and Gostin (2017) employ a public health advocacy lens in their editorial The Untapped Power of Soda Taxes, framing sugary beverages as a significant driver of obesity and non-communicable diseases. The language is urgent yet evidence-based, describing soda taxes as “powerful interventions” and integrating peer-reviewed research, such as Colchero et al.’s (2016) observational study in Mexico, to underscore health risks. The authors target policymakers and public health advocates, leveraging data from early adopters to argue for taxes as a tool to reduce consumption, generate revenue, and force corporate reformulation.

Regulatory Discourse

The U.S. Food and Drug Administration's (2024) updated “healthy” claim guidelines present a nuanced, science-based approach to labeling that neither fully condemns nor endorses UPFs, instead focusing strictly on nutrient profiles. The language is neutral and technical, emphasizing alignment with current dietary guidelines while actively avoiding terms like “processed” or “ultra-processed.” Health risks are addressed indirectly through limits on added sugars, sodium, and saturated fat, but the text omits discussion of processing methods, additives, or broader epidemiological links between UPFs and chronic diseases. This regulatory ambiguity is itself a rhetorical act: by declining to name “ultra-processing” as a category of concern, the FDA implicitly legitimizes the industry’s preferred framing and leaves the discursive terrain heavily contested.

Limitations

While this rhetorical analysis provides critical insight into public-facing UPF discourse, there are limitations to this qualitative approach. The study relies primarily on accessible marketing materials, advocacy reports, and published guidelines, meaning it does not capture internal corporate communications, private lobbying efforts, or proprietary platform data. Furthermore, the analysis prioritizes textual and visual rhetoric over consumer-level fieldwork; thus, it cannot definitively measure how diverse audiences interpret these conflicting narratives in real-time purchasing decisions. Future research would benefit from direct interviews and cross-cultural user testing to better understand the immediate impact of this rhetoric on consumers.

Conclusion

The analysis conducted across these artifacts reveals a consistent pattern: stakeholders use data selectively to support pre-existing agendas, and the rhetorical strategies they deploy construct fundamentally divergent accounts of the same products. Medical experts highlight health risks through precautionary language, while the food industry employs strategic ambiguity and euphemism to downplay those concerns. Advocacy groups leverage urgent, adversarial framing to galvanize action, while regulatory bodies utilize neutral, technical constructions to navigate industry pressures.

Industry’s co-optation of “health” rhetoric to resist regulation is perhaps the most consequential finding. By framing UPFs as essential components of modern diets through reformulation narratives, the industry effectively shifts the focus away from processing methods and metabolic risk, complicating regulatory efforts. Fostering collaboration among stakeholders to develop strategies that prioritize transparency, consumer education, and evidence-based policy is essential for creating a food environment that serves public health rather than obscuring it.
