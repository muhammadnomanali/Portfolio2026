Social Media Activism in Pakistan

A Study of Aristotelian Rhetoric and Multimodal Digital Practices

This study examines the multifaceted dimensions of digital activism in Pakistan, analyzing how rhetorical principles function in the construction of credibility and political discourse within a censored public sphere.

Abstract

Social media activism in Pakistan has emerged as a transformative force, empowering marginalized groups and resisting authoritarianism through the strategic deployment of Aristotelian rhetoric and multimodal digital practices. This study examines the multifaceted dimensions of digital activism in Pakistan, analyzing how rhetorical principles—logos, ethos, and pathos—function in the construction of credibility, the mobilization of support, and the fostering of political discourse within a censored public sphere. Multimodal practices integrating text, images, video, and interactive elements are shown to enhance both the inclusivity and the persuasive impact of activist communication. The study further investigates challenges posed by authoritarian censorship, including algorithmic suppression and the manipulation of online discourse, while highlighting adaptive strategies such as VPNs, encrypted communication, and multimodal circumvention tactics. Case studies of Pakistani digital commentators Imran Riaz Khan and Dr. Moeed Pirzada illustrate the application of classical rhetoric to contemporary YouTube-based activism. Platforms such as Twitter are critically analyzed for their role in amplifying activist voices and shaping public opinion, alongside the corrosive implications of polarization and misinformation for democratic participation. Automated frameworks for censorship detection and the strategic use of multimodal literacy are identified as pivotal instruments for safeguarding the integrity of public discourse. Ultimately, this study foregrounds the dynamic interplay between rhetoric, technology, and activism, offering directions for future research and advocacy aimed at empowering marginalized communities within Pakistan’s contested public sphere.

Introduction

The rise of social media has fundamentally transformed the landscape of activism globally, providing platforms through which marginalized voices can articulate grievances, organize collective action, and contest entrenched power structures. In Pakistan, where traditional forms of dissent have long faced severe repression—ranging from media blackouts to enforced disappearances—social media has become a vital channel for political expression and civic mobilization. This review examines the intricate dimensions of social media activism in Pakistan, with particular focus on two interrelated analytical frames: Aristotelian rhetoric and multimodal digital practices. By investigating how Pakistani activists deploy the classical appeals of logos, ethos, and pathos through digitally mediated, multimodal communication, this study illuminates the strategies by which they navigate a censored public sphere to construct credibility, foster discourse, and promote social change.

The significance of this inquiry extends beyond the Pakistani context. It contributes to a broader scholarly understanding of digital resistance in authoritarian settings, where state control over information flows is increasingly sophisticated and pervasive. As platforms like Twitter, YouTube, and Facebook become primary arenas for political contestation, the rhetorical and communicative strategies activists employ demand sustained critical attention. This study argues that Aristotelian rhetorical principles, far from being relics of classical antiquity, remain remarkably operative in digital environments, where credibility must be rapidly constructed, emotional appeals must resonate across heterogeneous audiences, and evidence-based argumentation must withstand algorithmic manipulation and state-sponsored disinformation. The multimodal character of digital platforms further complicates and enriches these dynamics, enabling activists to construct layered, affectively powerful narratives that transcend the limitations of text alone.

The study proceeds through seven substantive sections. Following this introduction, a theoretical framework establishes the conceptual foundations of Aristotelian rhetoric, multimodality, and the dynamics of authoritarianism and censorship. Subsequent sections examine the landscape of social media activism in Pakistan; the application of Aristotelian rhetoric in digital activism, including detailed case studies of Pakistani YouTube commentators Imran Riaz Khan and Dr. Moeed Pirzada; multimodal digital practices and their role in enhancing communication and engagement; resistance to authoritarianism and censorship; and the construction of credibility within the digital public sphere. The conclusion synthesizes these findings and identifies directions for future research.

Theoretical Framework

Before examining the specific dynamics of Pakistani digital activism, it is necessary to establish the conceptual apparatus that informs this study. The theoretical framework draws on three interrelated bodies of scholarship: social media as political participation, Aristotelian rhetoric as a model for persuasion, and multimodality as a communicative strategy. Together, these frameworks provide the analytical lens through which the subsequent empirical and case-study sections are interpreted.

Social Media Activism as Political Participation

Social media platforms have become essential instruments for political participation and civil society engagement, particularly in restrictive environments such as Pakistan. These platforms facilitate collaboration between users and systems, enhancing information-seeking and political discourse (Oviatt & Soulier, 2020). Their influence extends to domains such as corporate reputation and investor behavior, illustrating the breadth of their impact on public opinion and collective action (Gomez-Carrasco & Michelon, 2017). By democratizing access to political discussion, social media allows diverse voices to contribute to public deliberation, challenging the gatekeeping functions of traditional media (Yılmaz, 2017). Civil society organizations, including Amnesty International, have strategically leveraged these technologies to amplify advocacy and mobilization (Özkula et al., 2021). In Pakistan specifically, social media activism has provided marginalized groups with alternative avenues for political discourse and rights assertion, transforming localized grievances into global movements through hashtags and viral campaigns.

Beyond instrumental communication, social media fosters collective identity and solidarity—elements crucial for sustained activism. Activists share personal narratives that create emotional connections, bolster movement credibility, and encourage broader participation. As platforms evolve, their role in political engagement and civil society warrants ongoing scrutiny, particularly regarding their capacity to challenge traditional power dynamics and offer marginalized populations new channels for influence. This participatory potential, however, depends on the rhetorical strategies activists use to construct credible and persuasive messages—a question that directs attention to Aristotelian rhetoric.

Aristotelian Rhetoric: Foundations for Credibility and Persuasion

Aristotelian rhetoric, encompassing logos (logical reasoning), ethos (speaker credibility), and pathos (emotional appeal), provides a robust framework for analyzing how persuasion operates in social media activism. These elements illuminate the construction and reception of arguments, particularly in politically charged contexts (Stucki & Sager, 2018). In digital activism, where polarization is pervasive, ethos and pathos prove especially vital for engaging audiences and garnering support, though their systematic deployment in online discussions remains underexplored (Gajewska et al., 2024). By applying Aristotelian rhetoric, activists navigate the complexities of digital communication, enhancing message impact and audience engagement (Saaty, 2020). Understanding these rhetorical foundations is essential for activists seeking to create messages that both inform and inspire collective action. Yet rhetoric alone does not account for the full range of communicative resources available in digital environments; it must be understood alongside the multimodal affordances of the platforms on which it is deployed.

Multimodality in Digital Activism

Multimodal practices are central to effective communication in digital activism, utilizing diverse media forms to convey complex messages. The integration of text, audio, visual, and physiological data enriches group communication dynamics (Lange et al., 2024), catering to varied audience preferences and making activism more inclusive. This approach aligns with educational strategies emphasizing multimodal input for improved engagement (Oviatt & Soulier, 2020). Visual literacy, in particular, enables activists to craft meaningful narratives that resonate across cultural and ideological divides (Eisenmann & Meyer, 2018). Platforms such as Twitter demonstrate this multimodal potential, integrating visual and audio elements to enhance rhetorical awareness (Saaty, 2020). As digital activism evolves, multimodal strategies remain integral to crafting persuasive, accessible messages that empower marginalized groups. These communicative possibilities, however, operate within a political context that actively seeks to constrain them—a tension that necessitates attention to the dynamics of authoritarianism and censorship.

Authoritarianism and Censorship: Challenges for Marginalized Groups

In Pakistan, authoritarian governance and censorship pose significant challenges to marginalized groups seeking political participation. “Censorship leakage,” wherein network tools deployed for domestic censorship produce international ramifications, illustrates the far-reaching consequences of these practices (Cho et al., 2017). Such measures suppress dissent and distort online narratives, while cybertroopers exacerbate discourse polarization and hinder democratic engagement (Akinnubi & Agarwal, 2023). Paradoxically, state censorship efforts often fail to restrict information access, sometimes popularizing the very content they seek to suppress (Nabi, 2014). Social media attempts to control narratives can inadvertently amplify targeted information, compounded by misinformation and algorithmic bias (Kausar et al., 2021).

Dependence on traditional media, often curtailed during periods of political unrest, underscores the necessity of alternative platforms for information gathering and dissemination (Mayeesha et al., 2018). Military rule and ethno-linguistic and religious conflicts further fragment the political landscape, weakening collective action among marginalized groups (Baqir et al., 2023). Although social media activism offers resistance avenues, skepticism persists regarding its substantive efficacy, often dismissed as “slacktivism” (Yılmaz, 2017). Automatic censorship detection in microblogs, analyzing communication graphs disrupted by censorship actions, represents an increasingly relevant area of inquiry (Morrison, 2014). Understanding the interplay between authoritarianism, censorship, and digital activism is crucial for developing strategies that empower marginalized groups and foster a democratic public sphere.

Social Media Activism in Pakistan

Having established the theoretical apparatus, this section examines how social media activism has developed in Pakistan, tracing its evolution, identifying key actors and platforms, and analyzing its impact on political participation. The Pakistani case is particularly instructive because it demonstrates how activists deploy the rhetorical and multimodal strategies discussed above within a concrete sociopolitical environment characterized by democratic instability, military interference, and systematic censorship.

Evolution of Social Media Activism

Social media activism in Pakistan is closely intertwined with the country’s political and social transformations. Platforms like Twitter have gained particular prominence during political crises, reflecting and intensifying societal polarization while shaping public opinion (Haq et al., 2023; Baqir et al., 2023). This mode of digital engagement has redefined political activism and prompted civil society organizations to adapt their strategies to leverage new communicative tools (Özkula et al., 2021). Twitter trends offer real-time insight into unfolding events, empowering individuals and groups to mobilize support and advocate for change with unprecedented speed (Kausar et al., 2021). The historical trajectory of social media activism in Pakistan reflects a broader trend toward digital empowerment, offering marginalized groups new avenues to challenge authoritarian practices and assert their rights.

Key Players and Platforms

Social media activism in Pakistan involves diverse actors—political figures, civil society organizations, and grassroots activists—utilizing various digital platforms to shape discourse. An analysis of Twitter data from 160 politicians highlights the role of political actors in driving discourse and influencing public opinion (Baqir et al., 2023). Civil society organizations and grassroots movements leverage platforms including Twitter, Facebook, and Instagram to challenge authoritarian practices and advocate for social change (Özkula et al., 2021). Twitter serves as a particularly central platform, facilitating rapid information dissemination and public engagement, though challenges such as the manipulation of trends persist, as frameworks like Manipify demonstrate in their analysis of user behavior patterns (Kausar et al., 2021).

Mobilization and Awareness Strategies

Pakistani activists employ strategies that leverage digital platforms to bypass traditional hierarchies, assert their voices, and challenge authoritarian structures (Özkula et al., 2021). Hashtags and trending topics serve as focal points for discourse, amplifying messages and fostering collective action (Kausar et al., 2021). Multimodal content—integrating text, images, and video—enriches communication and deepens engagement (Saaty, 2020). Innovative strategies, including the data mining of personal stories and the adaptation of organizational logics, sustain activism’s effectiveness and amplify its impact on social change (Yılmaz, 2017; Özkula et al., 2021; Mayeesha et al., 2018; Gomez-Carrasco & Michelon, 2017).

Impact on Political Participation

Social media activism has profoundly reshaped political participation in Pakistan, altering engagement dynamics among political actors, civil society, and the public. Platforms like Twitter enable participation in conversations that challenge traditional power structures (Baqir et al., 2023). Activists and political figures engage directly with audiences, fostering low-cost mass mobilization and greater inclusivity (Yılmaz, 2017; Baqir et al., 2023; Özkula et al., 2021; Mayeesha et al., 2018). However, challenges such as trend manipulation threaten public perception and engagement integrity, as malicious actors exploit trending topics to mislead audiences in politically charged environments (Saaty, 2020; Baqir et al., 2023; Mayeesha et al., 2018; Cobbe, 2021; Kausar et al., 2021). Despite these obstacles, the strategic use of social media continues to empower individuals and groups, contributing to a more inclusive political landscape.

Aristotelian Rhetoric in Digital Activism

This section examines how Aristotelian rhetorical principles operate in practice, first through a theoretical discussion of emotional narratives and political framing, and then through detailed case studies of two prominent Pakistani YouTube commentators: Imran Riaz Khan and Dr. Moeed Pirzada. These case studies provide concrete evidence that classical rhetoric—adapted to digital platforms and Pakistani sociolinguistic contexts—remains a powerful instrument for constructing credibility and mobilizing publics.

Framework for Emotional Narratives

Constructing emotional narratives is crucial in digital activism for engaging audiences and garnering support. Aristotelian rhetoric, particularly ethos and pathos, provides a framework for these narratives, enabling activists to appeal simultaneously to the ethical character and emotions of their audiences. The cooperative framework in conversational search technologies, which emphasizes user intent and interaction modes, parallels emotional narrative frameworks in digital activism by focusing on audience engagement and tailoring messages for emotional resonance (Oviatt & Soulier, 2020).

Bloggers play diverse roles in shaping discourse and emotional narratives, categorizable as cybertroopers, commentator bloggers, and congressional bloggers (Akinnubi & Agarwal, 2023). Each category deploys distinct rhetorical strategies: cybertroopers employ emotional appeals to rally partisan support, while commentator bloggers establish credibility through factual reporting. The PolarIs method, which collects and annotates discussions on polarizing issues with attention to rhetorical strategies, further highlights the importance of ethos and pathos in crafting emotionally compelling narratives (Gajewska et al., 2024). Organizational change theories within civil society organizations reveal how social media logics influence internal structures and cultures, emphasizing emotional narratives as instruments of audience engagement (Özkula et al., 2021). Research has also developed tools for resilient communication during crises, aiding activists in constructing robust emotional narratives (Brussee & Pouwelse, 2015).

Enhancing Political Framing with Aristotelian Categories

Aristotelian rhetorical categories—logos, ethos, and pathos—enhance political messaging in digital activism by providing a structured apparatus for argument construction. This framework enables political actors to craft messages integrating logical reasoning, credibility construction, and emotional appeal. Evidence-based arguments increase credibility, which is essential in direct-democratic campaigns where persuasion is paramount (Saaty, 2020; Gajewska et al., 2024; Stucki & Sager, 2018; Cobbe, 2021; Mayeesha et al., 2018).

Logos allows activists to construct arguments grounded in facts, fostering credibility and rational engagement. Ethos establishes the authenticity and reliability essential to gaining audience support in polarized discussions. Pathos mobilizes support and fosters solidarity, creating urgency for action. Ethnographic evidence from Amnesty International illustrates social media activism’s transformative influence on organizing logics within civil society organizations, necessitating rhetorical strategies that align with digital pressures (Özkula et al., 2021). Strategically deploying these categories enables activists to navigate fragmented online spaces, ensuring messages resonate across demographic and ideological divides. This approach cultivates a more inclusive public sphere in which marginalized voices can assert rights and challenge authoritarian practices, particularly where traditional media is censored (Baqir et al., 2023; Mayeesha et al., 2018).

Case Study: Imran Riaz Khan’s YouTube Channel

Imran Riaz Khan represents a paradigmatic case of populist digital rhetoric in Pakistan. His YouTube channel, which has attracted millions of views, deploys Aristotelian rhetorical strategies in ways that are at once culturally specific and analytically generalizable. The following analysis examines his construction of ethos, his deployment of pathos, his sensitivity to kairos, and his strategic deconstruction of opponents’ credibility.

Establishing Ethos. Khan adopts a solemn tone and direct eye contact with the camera, mirroring Aristotle’s emphasis on phronesis (practical wisdom) by positioning himself as a knowledgeable commentator on political injustices. His deployment of religious and nationalist rhetoric (“Allah, Pakistan, and Justice”) reinforces his aretê (virtue), aligning with Aristotle’s principle that a speaker’s moral character strengthens persuasive appeal. By referencing his past sacrifices—imprisonment and media blackouts—Khan constructs an ethos of eunoia (goodwill), signaling that his activism serves the public good rather than personal gain.

Emotional Appeal and Stylistic Choices. Khan’s deliberate pauses and controlled anger evoke a judicial ethos, akin to Aristotle’s notion that a credible speaker modulates emotion to appear both rational and passionate. Visual imagery of Khan amidst crowds of supporters amplifies his ethos through popular validation—a modern parallel to Aristotle’s argument that public endorsement reinforces trustworthiness.

Kairos and Contextual Discourse. Released during periods of political crisis, Khan’s clips exemplify kairos (timeliness): his ethos gains potency by addressing urgent public grievances, fulfilling Aristotle’s criterion that persuasion depends on seizing the “right moment.” His use of vernacular language and local idioms (e.g., “Qaum ka maal,” the national exchequer belongs to the people) adapts classical ethos to Pakistan’s sociolinguistic context, demonstrating that Aristotle’s framework transcends cultural boundaries.

Contrasting Ethos. Khan’s sarcastic mimicry of political elites—mocking their “gentrified Urdu”—deconstructs opponents’ ethos by exposing perceived hypocrisy, a tactic Aristotle endorsed for disputing an adversary’s character. By juxtaposing his grassroots imagery with clips of corrupt politicians, Khan employs visual antithesis to amplify his ethical superiority.

Bridging Classical Theory to Digital Practice. Khan’s YouTube activism redefines Aristotelian ethos for the digital age: his screen presence replaces the Athenian agora, yet the core principles—credibility, virtue, and communal goodwill—remain unchanged. His viral clips demonstrate how digital platforms now mediate ethos, proving classical rhetoric’s enduring relevance in shaping public opinion. Case Study: Dr. Moeed Pirzada’s YouTube Channel

If Khan represents the populist orator, Dr. Moeed Pirzada embodies the philosopher-rhetorician. His YouTube channel deploys a markedly different ethos—one grounded in academic authority, analytical detachment, and institutional knowledge. The contrast between these two figures illustrates the range of rhetorical strategies available within Pakistan’s digital public sphere.

Establishing Ethos. Pirzada’s ethos is anchored in phronesis, evident in his methodical breakdowns of geopolitical systems and policy critiques. Unlike emotive activists, he cites historical precedents (Cold War analogies) and institutional data (IMF reports, judicial records) to position himself as a dispassionate analyst—a deliberate appeal to logos that paradoxically strengthens his ethos as a trustworthy intellectual. His aretê is signaled through restrained diction and a professorial tone. References to his academic pedigree (Columbia University, policy advisory roles) and apolitical branding (“I serve ideas, not parties”) construct eunoia, framing his activism as scholarly duty rather than partisan advocacy.

Emotional Appeal and Stylistic Choices. Pirzada’s pathos is subtle but potent, leveraging Socratic questioning (“What does the Constitution actually say?”) and irony to evoke indignation at systemic failures. Visual aids—on-screen charts, archival footage—serve as “rational pathos,” using evidence to amplify outrage while preserving his ethos as a detached observer. Unlike Khan’s crowd imagery, Pirzada’s solitary framing within a book-lined studio visually reinforces his ethos as a solitary truth-seeker.

Kairos and Contextual Discourse. Pirzada’s kairos lies in the timing of “explainer” videos during moments of institutional crisis—post-election constitutional debates, for instance. By dissecting why events unfold rather than merely what happened, he fulfills Aristotle’s criterion of seizing the “teachable moment.” His use of anglophone legal jargon (sine qua non of governance) alongside Urdu paraphrases (sarkari dhandli, official rigging) adapts classical ethos to Pakistan’s elite–public divide.

Contrasting Ethos. Pirzada deconstructs opponents’ ethos through reductio ad absurdum, dissecting their claims with forensic precision. He employs academic scorn, quoting Hobbes or Rousseau to highlight leaders’ philosophical illiteracy. His split-screen comparisons of politicians’ past promises against present failures serve as visual enthymemes, allowing logic to undermine credibility without overt polemics.

Bridging Classical Theory to Digital Practice. Pirzada’s vlogs reimagine the Athenian lyceum for YouTube: his studio becomes a digital agora where policy is debated with Aristotelian rigor. Where Khan revives the orator, Pirzada embodies the philosopher-rhetorician, demonstrating that ethos can be constructed through syllogisms as effectively as through sacrifice. His fusion of TED Talk aesthetics with grassroots advocacy challenges Western models of digital ethos, showing how erudition itself becomes resistance under censorship. Ethos and Pathos in Polarizing Discussions

In polarizing discussions, the strategic deployment of ethos and pathos is crucial for engaging audiences and sustaining discourse. Ethos establishes trust and authority; evidence enhances this trustworthiness, making credibility a potent instrument in contentious environments (Stucki & Sager, 2018). Pathos mobilizes support and fosters solidarity, yet evidence does not necessarily enhance emotional appeal, requiring activists to balance credibility and affective resonance. The digitalization of social interactions necessitates reevaluation of traditional concepts related to trust and polarization. Recent research reveals patterns in activist deployment of ethos and pathos across diverse political landscapes, including Twitter discourse in Pakistan, underscoring the need for computational tools to analyze large datasets and deepen understanding of dynamics in polarizing exchanges (Baqir et al., 2023; Gajewska et al., 2024; Mayeesha et al., 2018).

By leveraging ethos for trust and pathos for emotional connection, activists craft persuasive narratives that resonate amid contentious debates. This dual approach strengthens the persuasive power of activist messaging and contributes to a more inclusive public sphere in which diverse voices engage in dialogue and challenge authoritarian practices.

Resilient Communication and Rhetorical Strategies

Resilient communication strategies in digital activism depend on the effective deployment of Aristotelian rhetoric to navigate the complexities of online discourse. Gajewska et al.’s (2024) systematic approach captures the intricacies of rhetorical appeal, offering a robust framework for activist communication that addresses existing methodological shortcomings. By integrating logos, ethos, and pathos, activists construct persuasive narratives that withstand pressures and maintain audience engagement. This approach facilitates informed discourse, particularly in polarizing discussions where evidence, cultural symbols, and emotional appeals shape audience perception (Stucki & Sager, 2018; Gajewska et al., 2024).

Resilient strategies also address challenges of misinformation and bias endemic to digital platforms. The systematic analysis of rhetorical appeals enables activists to develop credible and emotionally resonant messages that counteract distorted narratives. Personal stories and evidence-based arguments enhance trustworthiness and engagement, fostering inclusive discourse that addresses media censorship and societal polarization (Stucki & Sager, 2018; Gajewska et al., 2024; Saaty, 2020; Mayeesha et al., 2018). As digital activism evolves, the strategic application of resilient communication remains integral to empowering marginalized groups and resisting authoritarian practices. Having examined the rhetorical dimension of digital activism, the analysis now turns to the multimodal practices that serve as the material substrate for these rhetorical strategies.

Multimodal Digital Practices

Rhetorical strategies do not operate in a communicative vacuum. The persuasive force of logos, ethos, and pathos in digital environments depends fundamentally on the multimodal affordances of the platforms through which they are expressed. This section examines how the integration of text, images, video, and interactive elements enhances the effectiveness of activist communication, extending the rhetorical analysis of the preceding section into the domain of media and platform design.

Integration of Multimodal Inputs

The integration of multimodal inputs is pivotal for constructing cohesive narratives in digital activism. Combining media forms—audio, visual, and biosensing data—enhances the richness and accessibility of communication, enabling activists to transcend the limitations of text-based approaches. Categorizing methods based on captured data types underscores the need for integrated analytical techniques to process and synthesize these inputs effectively (Lange et al., 2024). Advances in multimodal literacy, originally prominent in educational settings, have proven highly applicable to digital activism, enabling the decoding and construction of intricate narratives (Eisenmann & Meyer, 2018). Text, images, videos, and other media forms are strategically combined to enhance message clarity and engagement across platforms. Visual elements simplify complex ideas while audio components add emotional depth, collectively empowering marginalized groups (Baqir et al., 2023; Mayeesha et al., 2018; Eisenmann & Meyer, 2018; Oviatt & Soulier, 2020; Lange et al., 2024).

Visual Literacy and Multimodal Learning

Visual literacy underpins the creation and interpretation of multimodal content, serving as a critical competency for conveying complex ideas effectively across platforms. It enables activists to decode and construct visual narratives that resonate emotionally and intellectually with diverse audiences (Eisenmann & Meyer, 2018). By integrating text, images, videos, and other media, activists craft compelling narratives that attract attention, foster emotional connections, and inspire action. The rise of social media has transformed traditional organizational principles within civil society, facilitating new forms of connective action and mobilization. Data-driven activism, particularly the analysis of personal stories, reveals emotional impacts and highlights key actors within social movements. In contexts where censorship limits traditional data collection, multimodal strategies leveraging visual literacy offer innovative means to amplify activist voices (Saaty, 2020; Eisenmann & Meyer, 2018; Özkula et al., 2021; Mayeesha et al., 2018).

Application of Twitter’s Affordances

Twitter’s unique features provide a dynamic platform for multimodal digital activism, enabling activists to disseminate information, engage audiences, and advocate for social change with considerable speed. The platform’s real-time capabilities allow rapid message dissemination and timely responses to unfolding events, fostering collective action across diverse communities. Hashtags and trending topics amplify visibility, focusing discourse on specific issues and mobilizing support (Kausar et al., 2021). Twitter supports multimedia content, enabling activists to enrich their narratives through visual storytelling and multimedia integration, strengthening emotional resonance and making messages more likely to inspire solidarity and action (Saaty, 2020). Despite challenges such as trend manipulation, frameworks like Manipify provide tools to detect and analyze manipulative behaviors, ensuring the integrity of activist campaigns (Kausar et al., 2021). The strategic deployment of Twitter’s affordances empowers activists to bypass traditional hierarchies and engage in participatory advocacy. Yet these multimodal possibilities are precisely what authoritarian regimes seek to constrain—a dynamic examined in the following section.

Resistance to Authoritarianism and Censorship

The rhetorical and multimodal strategies examined in the preceding sections operate within a political environment that actively seeks to suppress them. This section analyzes the mechanisms of censorship in Pakistan, the strategies activists employ to circumvent them, the specific role of multimodal practices in evading detection, and the broader impact of censorship on public discourse and democratic engagement.

Censorship Mechanisms and Their Implications

Censorship in Pakistan involves a range of technical and structural strategies aimed at controlling information flow and silencing dissent. Autonomous Systems (ASes) play a central role, employing techniques such as DNS anomalies and TCP reset injections to block access to content and platforms. These interventions disrupt communication networks, suppress free expression, and narrow the diversity of viewpoints accessible to the public (Cho et al., 2017). DNS- and HTTP-level filtering further exemplifies state control over digital spaces, limiting the visibility of dissenting voices and shaping political narratives (Nabi, 2013). The emergence of monopolistic hubs within communication networks exacerbates these issues, concentrating information control and marginalizing underrepresented groups (Shirazi et al., 2013).

Censorship also alters the structural dynamics of communication networks. Topological analyses of communication graphs reveal disruptions in information flow and connectivity (Morrison, 2014). The manipulation of online discourse, particularly through human-operated accounts, distorts public perception and undermines social media’s integrity as a platform for genuine dialogue (Kausar et al., 2021). Paradoxically, state censorship often backfires through the Streisand Effect, in which attempts to suppress information amplify its visibility and galvanize resistance (Morrison, 2014; Mayeesha et al., 2018; Nabi, 2014).

Strategies for Bypassing Censorship

Activists in Pakistan employ diverse strategies to bypass censorship, leveraging both technical tools and adaptive practices. Public VPNs and web proxies are widely used to circumvent state-imposed filters, ensuring anonymity and access to restricted content (Nabi, 2013). Boolean network tomography and global censorship measurement platforms enable the identification of ASes responsible for censorship, informing targeted mitigation efforts (Cho et al., 2017). Communication graph analysis further enhances automated censorship detection (Morrison, 2014). The ongoing “cat-and-mouse” dynamic between censors and anti-censorship practitioners demands continuous innovation. Activists combine grassroots tactics with formalized resistance strategies—encrypted communication, decentralized platforms, and strategic information dissemination—to evade detection and maintain advocacy (Cobbe, 2021). Social media logics have reshaped traditional organizational structures, enabling civil society organizations to adopt more networked and responsive frameworks (Özkula et al., 2021). Models incorporating varying degrees of information transparency provide additional insights, as analyzing how transparency influences the emergence of monopolistic hubs allows activists to identify and exploit vulnerabilities within censorship frameworks (Shirazi et al., 2013).

Role of Multimodal Practices in Evading Censorship

Multimodal practices are increasingly vital for resisting censorship, as the integration of diverse media forms—text, images, audio, and video—complicates traditional censorship mechanisms. By diversifying communication formats, activists obscure the intent of their messages, making detection by censorship algorithms more difficult and enhancing the resilience of their efforts. Language and symbols play a strategic role in evading automated filters, with activists adapting communication styles to bypass detection (Cobbe, 2021). Research demonstrates that censorship often inadvertently amplifies restricted content, as suppression efforts increase visibility and mobilize public support (Nabi, 2014). Technical barriers such as DNS injection highlight the need for innovative strategies; multimodal practices reduce reliance on single communication modes, decreasing detection risks (Morrison, 2014). This connection between multimodality and censorship resistance underscores a key argument of this study: multimodal practices are not merely communicative enhancements but strategic instruments for sustaining activism under repressive conditions.

Impact of Censorship on Public Discourse

Censorship profoundly impacts public discourse in Pakistan, suppressing dissent and controlling narratives within the public sphere. These practices marginalize voices, distort dialogue, and undermine democratic engagement (Cho et al., 2017). Political polarization is exacerbated by manipulated narratives on social media, hindering constructive dialogue and entrenching divisions (Baqir et al., 2023). Algorithmic censorship further complicates public discourse by imposing biases that shape content visibility and framing, raising concerns about transparency and accountability (Stucki & Sager, 2018). Algorithmic decision-making limits diverse perspectives and erodes trust in digital platforms as spaces for open dialogue (Kausar et al., 2021). Censorship also fosters misinformation, as exemplified by manipulated Twitter trends that distort public perception and deepen divisions. Addressing these dynamics requires prioritizing inclusivity and resilience, ensuring digital platforms remain spaces for meaningful discourse.

Algorithmic Censorship and Its Impact on Communication

Algorithmic censorship poses significant challenges to communication integrity, particularly in authoritarian contexts where advanced filtering mechanisms suppress dissent. In Pakistan, DNS injection disrupts access to targeted content, impeding information flow and meaningful dialogue (Nabi, 2013). Algorithmic biases exacerbate polarization and hinder engagement on contentious issues, as observed in discussions on COVID-19 vaccines, climate change, and the US 2016 elections (Gajewska et al., 2024). The lack of transparency in algorithmic moderation diminishes trust in digital platforms, necessitating alternative approaches. Decentralized platforms prioritizing privacy and free expression, alongside legal frameworks to regulate algorithmic censorship, are essential for fostering inclusive communication spaces (Cobbe, 2021). As algorithmic censorship evolves, its impact on communication and public discourse demands critical examination, particularly regarding the interplay between technical filtering, algorithmic biases, and sociopolitical contexts (Mayeesha et al., 2018; Cobbe, 2021). The cumulative effect of these censorship dynamics raises a fundamental question: how can activists construct and maintain credibility within a public sphere that is systematically distorted?

Credibility Construction and the Digital Public Sphere

The preceding analysis has demonstrated that Pakistani activists deploy Aristotelian rhetoric through multimodal digital practices within an environment of persistent state censorship and algorithmic suppression. This section examines the culminating dimension of this dynamic: how credibility is constructed and maintained within a digital public sphere that is simultaneously enabling and constraining. The discussion addresses trust and authenticity as rhetorical accomplishments, and then considers automated frameworks for detecting censorship as institutional safeguards for the integrity of public discourse.

Trust and Authenticity in Digital Activism

Trust and authenticity are central to establishing credibility in digital activism, where the rapid pace of information flow can simultaneously enhance and undermine campaign integrity. Platforms like Twitter are instrumental in fostering engagement and community, enhancing authenticity among users and activists (Saaty, 2020). This engagement transcends transactional interactions, involving the development of rhetorical skill to craft resonant messages for diverse audiences. In digital activism, trust hinges on consistent, transparent communication, where the speaker’s ethical character (ethos) is vital for persuasion and support. Activists must adeptly navigate online discourse complexities, where misinformation and bias threaten narrative integrity. Interactive platforms enhance authenticity and drive collective action, as demonstrated by data-driven activism and personal storytelling during media censorship. Social media dynamics have challenged traditional organizational structures, prompting organizations like Amnesty International to adopt networked strategies prioritizing connective action (Özkula et al., 2021; Mayeesha et al., 2018). The interplay of trust and authenticity shapes individual campaigns and broader societal perceptions of activism, urging activists to innovate continually as audiences grow more discerning.

Frameworks for Automated Censorship Detection

Developing automated censorship detection frameworks is vital for safeguarding the integrity of public discourse, particularly under authoritarian regimes employing sophisticated suppression mechanisms. These frameworks leverage technological advancements such as data mining and automated detection to analyze censorship dynamics, providing insights into information control and manipulation within digital networks. While traditional social media data may be limited by censorship itself, personal narratives can reveal emotional impacts and identify key entities in social movements. Understanding various censorship strategies—including DNS and HTTP blocking—enhances comprehension of information suppression methods, enabling the development of comprehensive monitoring and circumvention tools (Nabi, 2013; Morrison, 2014; Mayeesha et al., 2018).

Integrating multimodal data offers a nuanced understanding of communication patterns and media interactions (Lange et al., 2024). The Manipify framework exemplifies the potential of automated detection systems, identifying human and bot manipulators on platforms like Twitter (Kausar et al., 2021). This comprehensive approach to Twitter trend analysis aids censorship detection and credibility construction by identifying manipulated narratives and maintaining the quality of public discourse. Examining information transparency’s role in network dynamics offers a novel perspective on censorship mitigation (Shirazi et al., 2013). Transparency deters censorship and encourages accountability among information providers, enhancing the health of public discourse. As censorship mechanisms evolve, refining and implementing automated detection frameworks remains essential for protecting digital communication environments. These frameworks empower activists and marginalized groups by safeguarding spaces for meaningful engagement and play a crucial role in resisting authoritarian practices (Cobbe, 2021; Özkula et al., 2021; Mayeesha et al., 2018).

Conclusion

This study has examined the dynamic interplay between Aristotelian rhetoric, multimodal digital practices, and social media activism in Pakistan’s contested public sphere. The analysis demonstrates that social media platforms have emerged as vital instruments for amplifying personal narratives and enhancing the visibility of protestors’ experiences, effectively conveying their struggles and emotions in the face of systematic censorship. The strategic application of Aristotelian rhetoric—especially ethos and pathos—plays a crucial role in bolstering the credibility and emotional resonance of activist communications, providing a durable framework for effective political discourse within digitally mediated environments.

The case studies of Imran Riaz Khan and Dr. Moeed Pirzada demonstrate the diverse rhetorical strategies through which classical principles are adapted to digital platforms and local sociopolitical contexts. Khan revives the figure of the populist orator, deploying nationalist imagery, vernacular language, and personal sacrifice narratives to construct ethos rooted in solidarity and communal goodwill. Pirzada, by contrast, embodies the philosopher-rhetorician, constructing ethos through academic authority, analytical detachment, and logos-driven critique. Together, these cases confirm that the Aristotelian triad of ethos, pathos, and logos remains remarkably productive for analyzing and practicing digital persuasion—a finding that extends beyond the Pakistani context to the broader study of digital activism under authoritarian conditions.

The multimodal character of digital platforms further enriches these rhetorical dynamics, enabling activists to construct layered, affectively powerful narratives that integrate text, video, images, and interactive elements. These multimodal practices are not merely communicative enhancements; they function as strategic instruments for sustaining activism under censorship, diversifying communication modes to evade detection and amplify reach. The polarization fostered by social media carries significant implications for governance and public policy, necessitating investigation into its effects on societal structures and democratic participation.

Social media activism should be recognized as a complementary force to traditional forms of activism, reflecting the fragmented and diverse nature of new social movements. As the landscape of digital activism continues to evolve, the insights developed here lay the groundwork for future research and advocacy, ensuring that marginalized groups can effectively assert their rights and confront authoritarian practices within the public sphere. Interdisciplinary collaboration—drawing on rhetoric, communication studies, political science, and computer science—is essential for cultivating a more nuanced comprehension of the challenges and opportunities presented by digital activism in promoting democratic engagement and social justice.
