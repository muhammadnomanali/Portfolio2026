Social Media Activism in Pakistan: A Study of Aristotelian Rhetoric and Multimodal Digital Practices

This project analyzes how Pakistani activists use multimodal digital practices to navigate state repression and algorithmic censorship, adapting classical rhetoric for high-risk environments.

Project Description

This project investigates how Pakistani social media activists utilize multimodal digital practices to contest state narratives and navigate platform censorship. Originally developed for Dr. Leigh Gruwell’s course on the histories of rhetoric, the project examines the utility and limitations of classical Aristotelian rhetoric in contemporary, high-risk digital environments. By analyzing YouTube and Twitter posts, the project highlights how activists construct credibility under suspicion, compress complex claims into memorable visual cues, and assemble evidence to counter official state claims. Ultimately, the project demonstrates that social media in Pakistan operates not merely as a communication tool, but as a critical site for rhetorical self-construction and historical witnessing under severe institutional and algorithmic pressure.

Rhetorical and Technical Decisions

Methodologically, the project triangulates a classical lens, a marginalized counterstory framework, and a digital/algorithmic reality. While Aristotle (2007) provides a baseline vocabulary for persuasion (ethos, pathos, logos), Hill (1958) reminds us of the specific civic assumptions underlying classical rhetoric—assumptions of stable forums and reciprocal debate that fail in contexts of extreme censorship. Therefore, my technical decision to use Aristotle was not to recenter the canon, but to establish a point of productive strain to show how traditional methods must adapt to modern digital constraints.

I made the rhetorical choice to focus heavily on publicly visible signals of algorithmic suppression, demonstrating how activists engineer redundancy through multimodal design (e.g., using stitched citations or platform switching when a video is removed). However, acknowledging the limitations of my approach was a crucial ethical decision. Relying strictly on surface texts risks obscuring private organizing channels and the distinct dangers faced by women and lower-bandwidth communities. Recognizing these limits directly answers Royster’s (2000) mandate that methodological choices are inseparable from ethical responsibility. Moving forward, this project suggests that writing histories of rhetoric requires keeping the canon in view, centering marginalized voices, and critically framing the platform itself.

Theoretical Connections

To situate this work within the broader fields of rhetorical historiography and cultural rhetorics, I moved beyond classical frameworks to embrace counterstory and borderland theories. As Martinez (2020) articulates, counterstory provides a critical methodology for reading activist feeds as real-time interventions against a Master Narrative that seeks to frame dissent as chaos or foreign manipulation. This historiographic lens reveals the platform feed as a scene of active historical writing. Furthermore, Anzaldúa’s (1987) concept of the borderlands accurately names the in-between condition of Pakistani activists who must constantly navigate between platform moderation rules and state laws, as well as between global legibility and local danger.

To ensure my analytical distance did not reproduce the marginalization I sought to critique, I applied Royster’s (2000) ethic of care, which guided my theoretical choices regarding evidence and tone. Cushman et al.'s (2019) call for pluriversal rhetorics further pushed the project to avoid measuring Pakistani digital practices against Western norms. Instead, this framework allowed me to recognize localized tactics—such as code-switching and intertextual Quranic appeals—as rhetorically complete on their own terms.

Within the realm of digital rhetoric, Eyman (2015) clarifies that persuasion is fundamentally shaped by interfaces, infrastructures, and protocols. Building on this, McKee and Porter’s (2017) work on digital media ethics helped me account for the layered governance of platform moderation and state surveillance that makes rhetorical uptake so precarious. Furthermore, Boyle et al. (2018) encouraged me to treat the digital not as an isolated sphere, but as deeply entangled with material risks such as detention and disappearance, thereby explaining activists' preference for short videos and mirrored uploads. Finally, Hart-Davidson’s (2020) concept of writing with robots provided the necessary vocabulary to analyze algorithms as non-human rhetorical actors that sort, throttle, and erase political speech.
