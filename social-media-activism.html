Social Media Activism in Pakistan: A Study of Aristotelian Rhetoric and Multimodal Digital Practices

Analyzes how social media activism in Pakistan adapts Aristotelian rhetoric through counterstory, multimodal strategies, and algorithm-driven digital environments.

Introduction: Classical Rhetoric as Productive Strain

Scholarship in rhetorical historiography has increasingly challenged canonical, Western-centered frameworks by emphasizing situated, marginalized, and multimodal practices of meaning-making. This project contributes to that conversation by examining social media activism in Pakistan through both classical rhetorical concepts and contemporary digital frameworks.

This meta-analysis reflects on how the project reframed my understanding of rhetorical history and method in Dr. Leigh Gruwell’s ENGL 7300: Histories of Rhetoric and Rhetorical Historiographies. I began with Aristotle not to re-center the canon but to establish a recognizable vocabulary for persuasion that continues to structure public discourse. Using ethos, pathos, and logos as a baseline allowed me to describe how Pakistani activists construct credibility under suspicion, narrate injury and hope through compressed multimodal forms, and assemble evidence to contest official claims.

At the same time, the civic assumptions underlying Aristotelian rhetoric do not hold in contexts where legacy media can function as a mechanism of erasure and where visibility itself carries risk. Stable forums, reciprocal debate, and relatively transparent power are not guaranteed in such conditions. As Hill’s scholarship on Aristotle suggests, classical rhetoric is always mediated through interpretation and context. In this project, Aristotle therefore becomes not a sufficient framework but a point of productive strain that reveals the limits of canonical rhetoric when applied to conditions of political precarity (Aristotle; Hill).

Historiographic Turn: Counterstory and Cultural Rhetorics

Building on this strain, the project turns toward counterstory and cultural rhetorics as historiographic methods. Drawing on Martinez’s theory of counterstory, I read activist social media feeds not simply as communication channels but as interventions against dominant narratives that frame dissent as disorder or foreign manipulation. These feeds function as sites of historical writing in real time where activists produce alternative archives that contest official accounts.

Anzaldúa’s concept of borderlands clarifies the positionality of Pakistani activists who operate between platform governance and state surveillance, between English and Urdu, and between global visibility and local vulnerability. This in-between condition shapes rhetorical strategy as well as audience design.

Royster’s work on disciplinary landscaping and ethical responsibility informs my methodological approach, particularly in attending to how analytical distance can reproduce marginalization. Rather than treating activist texts as objects of detached analysis, this project approaches them through an ethic of care. Similarly, Cushman, Keith, and Wilder’s call for pluriversal rhetorics encourages a shift away from evaluating Pakistani digital practices against Western norms. Localized strategies such as code-switching, intertextual appeals to religious and constitutional discourse, and kinship-based circulation are therefore understood as rhetorically complete within their own contexts (Martinez; Anzaldúa; Royster; Cushman et al.).

Digital Rhetoric: Platforms, Ethics, and Algorithmic Agency

The project then situates these historiographic commitments within the field of digital rhetoric. Eyman’s definition of digital rhetoric emphasizes that persuasion is shaped not only by language but also by interfaces, infrastructures, and protocols. This perspective is essential for understanding how activist communication operates within platform constraints.

McKee and Porter’s framework for digital media ethics highlights the layered nature of governance in online spaces, where platform moderation, state surveillance, and audience reporting intersect. These overlapping systems create conditions in which rhetorical uptake is uncertain and often precarious.

Boyle, Brown, and Ceraso complicate the distinction between digital and material realities by demonstrating how online rhetoric is entangled with physical risk, including detention, job loss, and disappearance. This helps explain why activists rely on short-form video, screenshot-based evidence, and mirrored uploads as strategies of resilience.

Hart-Davidson’s discussion of writing with algorithms provides a vocabulary for understanding non-human agency in rhetorical production. Algorithms do not merely distribute content. They actively shape visibility by sorting, throttling, and sometimes erasing political speech. Composing for publics in Pakistan therefore also entails composing for algorithmic systems that mediate circulation (Eyman; McKee & Porter; Boyle, Brown, & Ceraso; Hart-Davidson).

Methodological Framework: A Three-Part Analytical Structure

Methodologically, the project is organized through a three-part analytical framework consisting of the Classical Lens, the Counterstory and Cultural Rhetorics Framework, and the Digital and Algorithmic Context. This structure allows for a layered analysis that connects rhetorical theory to situated practice and positions the project within ongoing conversations in rhetorical historiography and digital rhetoric.

The classical lens provides a descriptive vocabulary for identifying persuasive strategies. The counterstory framework justifies centering Pakistan as a site of rhetorical knowledge production rather than treating it as a deviation from a Western norm. The digital framework accounts for how multimodal practices are shaped by platform affordances and constraints.

This triangulated approach enables close readings of social media artifacts, particularly YouTube and Twitter posts, by linking visual design and timing with credibility-building strategies and circulation tactics such as hashtags, subtitles, and platform migration. Rather than applying Aristotle to Pakistan, this method demonstrates how rhetorical categories are stretched, contested, and redefined under conditions of precarity.

Findings: Rhetorical Adaptation Under Constraint

Several key findings emerge from this analysis. First, ethos often precedes logos in contexts of repression. Activists’ lived experiences, including prior arrests, visible harassment, or documented risk, function as evidence of credibility and sincerity. Personal vulnerability becomes a rhetorical resource that supports persuasive force.

Second, pathos operates multimodally. Visual techniques such as slow panning shots, intercut archival footage, and direct-to-camera monologues compress complex arguments into emotionally resonant and easily shareable forms. These strategies are particularly effective in environments where attention is fragmented and content may be removed.

Third, logos remains central but is redistributed across formats. Evidence appears in screenshots, legal documents, data visualizations, and stitched media. These forms are designed for rapid circulation and replication, ensuring that information persists even when original posts are taken down. These patterns collectively illustrate how activists engage in real-time archival work and produce counter-histories that anticipate erasure (Aristotle; Martinez; Boyle, Brown, & Ceraso).

Limitations and Ethical Considerations

This project has several limitations. It focuses primarily on publicly available texts and does not account for private organizing spaces or encrypted communication channels. It also does not fully address how rhetorical practices differ across gender, religious identity, or access to digital infrastructure.

The analysis relies on observable indicators of algorithmic suppression rather than internal platform data, which limits the ability to make definitive claims about visibility. In addition, while the three-part framework provides analytical clarity, it may underrepresent tensions between ethical responsibility and analytical detail.

Following Royster’s emphasis on accountability, these limitations point toward future research directions, including interview-based studies with informed consent, comparative analyses across marginalized groups, and interdisciplinary collaborations to examine platform behavior more systematically (Royster; McKee & Porter; Hart-Davidson).

Conclusion: Rhetoric, History, and Platformed Resistance

This project reframes Aristotelian rhetoric as a partial but productive tool within a broader historiographic and digital ecology. Social media activism in Pakistan is not simply a tool for dissent. It is a site of rhetorical self-construction and historical witnessing under conditions of constraint.

Understanding activists as authors of counter-history and algorithms as shaping forces in circulation highlights both the possibilities and vulnerabilities of multimodal resistance. The project demonstrates that histories of rhetoric must be written with attention to the canon, with the margins at the center, and with the platform as a structuring force in rhetorical practice.
